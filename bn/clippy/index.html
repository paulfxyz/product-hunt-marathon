      SaaS is dead

      Rent by default?Not anymore.

      A provocation, not an obituary. My bet is that more companies will build and own the workflows they used to rent.

      Own the workflow. Question the recurring bill.

      Swapping SaaS seats for an uncontrolled AI bill isn't sovereignty. Use AI to create reusable tools, turn repeated prompts into owned apps, use ordinary code for predictable steps, and test small self-hosted models for narrow jobs.

      Compare cost per successful result, including hardware, power, hosting, licences, security, maintenance and human time. Self-hosting is not automatically cheaper or more private.

      Reproducible features are no longer a moat.

      My deliberately aggressive belief: AI can already reproduce the software functionality of a SaaS company's entire product line, for less than one month of its revenue. Soon that budget could fall to one customer's annual bill, then their monthly bill.

      If I'm right, SaaS economics built only on reproducible features could be reduced to dust. This is my prediction, not a benchmark. Recreating software is not rebuilding proprietary data, infrastructure, compliance or customer trust.

      • Build where ownership is an advantage. Internal tools, repeated workflows, anything your team touches every day.
      • Keep paying where it's worth it. When a supplier delivers value you can't sensibly reproduce: data, networks, regulated services.
      • Budget the whole life, not the prototype. Iteration, AI usage, maintenance, security and expertise all count.
      • Software on demand, responsibility included. Every tool needs an owner, tests and a way back.

        Field guide

        What I'd do again, step by step

        Six short guides, one for each stage of a launch run.

        01

        Plan the run

        • One launch per day, plus a spare day for when something slips.
        • Put the whole schedule in one calendar file and share it.
        • Build day one's kit carefully. Every later day copies it.
        02

        Write once, adapt everywhere

        • Write one source post, then adapt it: short and threaded for X, longer with a list for LinkedIn.
        • Count links the way each platform does. X counts every link as 23 characters.
        • Post by hand. Read each post once more in the platform's own composer.
        03

        Images that stay true

        • Retake screenshots after every fix, before you share anything.
        • One idea per slide. A loop of about eight seconds or less shows more than a long one.
        • Use a wide, sturdy font. Condensed type turns to mush at thumbnail size.
        04

        Launch day

        • Product Hunt days start at 00:01 Pacific time. Plan to be around for the first hours.
        • Open with an honest maker comment: what AI did, what it cost, what it doesn't do.
        • Reply to every comment. Ask for feedback, never for votes.
        05

        A repo people trust

        • A README with a screenshot, a one-line pitch and a quick start that works.
        • A licence (MIT is the simplest), topics and a social preview image.
        • Tests, even a few, and an honest list of limits.
        06

        After the launch

        • Log every published link. When a date moves, you know what to update.
        • Fix bugs first, then make the smallest useful change.
        • Thank the people who took the time to give feedback.
