SaaS is dead.

Rent by default? Not anymore.

My bet is that more companies will build and own the workflows they used to rent. But swapping SaaS seats for endless AI calls misses the point. Use AI to create reusable tools, then make every recurring cost earn its place.

For companies

Replacing subscriptions with an uncontrolled AI bill isn't sovereignty. I want a CHO to turn repeated prompts into owned apps, use ordinary code for predictable steps, and test small self-hosted models for narrow jobs. Compare cost per successful result, including hardware, power, hosting, licences, security, maintenance and human time. Self-hosting is not automatically cheaper or private.

For SaaS founders and investors

My deliberately aggressive belief: AI can already reproduce the software functionality of any SaaS company's entire product line, small or large, for less than one month of its revenue. Soon, perhaps sooner than we expect, I think that budget could fall to one customer's annual bill, then eventually their monthly bill. If I'm right, SaaS economics built only on reproducible features could be reduced to dust. This is my prediction, not an industry benchmark. Recreating software is not rebuilding proprietary data, infrastructure, compliance or customer trust.

Software on demand. Responsibility included.

Build where ownership creates an advantage. Keep paying where a supplier delivers value you cannot sensibly reproduce. This Marathon is a public experiment, not proof that every subscription should disappear.

What is a Chief Hacking Officer?

Why “hacking”?

Think life hacks and biohacks: finding a smarter route through a problem, not just cybersecurity. Shortcuts through friction, not around consent, security or quality.

A Chief Hacking Officer uses AI to make an organisation work faster and better: sovereign software, reusable workflows and tools people can maintain. One accountable lead or small team, backed by the right experts. You don't need to be a founder to grow into the role.

AI + the right people

Centralise ownership, budgets and evaluation, not every decision. Work with internal specialists and expert contractors. Keep useful AI access available, define permissions and make handoffs real. A CHO enables engineering, sales, marketing, legal and operations; nobody replaces all their expertise.

Build with AI. Run with judgement.

Turn repeated prompts into reusable workflows. Use ordinary code for predictable steps. Test small models for bounded tasks and bigger models for genuine gaps. Measure quality and full cost, including upkeep and human review. The deliverable is a supported tool with an owner, not a chat transcript.

A useful comparison: Paysend's CEO describes selective AI trials, expansion after measurable value, and a shared internal coding agent. Not a blanket ban to cut costs. Read the Paysend CEO interview

One home for your code

Put every tool’s code, tests, documentation and deployment recipes in organisation-owned Git repositories: GitHub, GitLab or, preferably, a self-hosted/local forge. One governed source of truth, with access controls, backups and mirrors, not one fragile server. Keep secrets and personal data out.

Private where needed. Open where useful.

Publish as much reusable, non-sensitive code as rights and security allow. Keep critical internals private. Clear licences, tests and documented releases let other organisations improve a shared foundation instead of rebuilding it.

Fork evidence, not promises.

Give your teams and AI assistants reviewed code, test results and known limitations to build on. Fork a relevant release, adapt it, validate it in your context and send useful improvements upstream. A shared starting point for people and AI-assisted work, not automatic proof of safety.

Tools you could ship

Start with a recurring bottleneck. These are examples, not a promise to build everything.

  • MarketingCampaign landing pages, experiment dashboards and reusable creative workflows.
  • SalesProposal builders, account-research tools and CRM handoff automations.
  • OperationsOnboarding portals, approval flows, vendor tracking and internal dashboards.
  • EngineeringRelease tooling, QA assistants, incident runbooks and developer utilities.
  • LegalContract intake, clause libraries and review workflows, with legal professionals owning the judgement.
  • Social mediaEditorial calendars, draft libraries, approval queues and performance dashboards. Humans approve what gets published.
  • Public releasesCustomer-facing apps, small utilities and selected open-source projects, with a real owner for support and improvements.

Impact, not output volume

Aim for less repetitive work, better handoffs and tools teams can maintain. Measure the result, include the full running cost, and keep testing, security and human approval in scope. Often the right decision is to build, own and keep it sovereign.
