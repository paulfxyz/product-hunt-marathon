    Chapter II

    Field notes from Product Hunt.

    An honest account from a long-time member: what worked, what didn't, and what I wish were different.

    A love letter, with a few corrections

    I've been on Product Hunt since its early days. Back then I was in a relationship with the first French journalist to interview Ryan Hoover, so I like to think I heard about it at the kitchen table before most of France did. I've browsed it, hunted on it and recommended it for a decade. This Marathon was my way of giving something back: eleven useful, free tools in a row, plus one for fun.

    The rules I set for myself were simple. No launch squad. No upvote ask. No paid engagement. Just one product a day, a clear page, a real maker comment and an invitation to send feedback.

    The editorial team didn't pick it up

    On Product Hunt, being live and being seen are two different things. Every launch gets a page, but the team hand-picks which ones appear on the homepage, and featuring is an editorial decision that meeting the guidelines doesn't guarantee. Launches that aren't featured sit behind a "See all of today's products" link that most visitors never click.

    As far as I could see, the Marathon never made it onto the editors' radar as a story. Eleven consecutive free, MIT-licensed launches from one maker read as eleven separate small products, each judged on its own day. I don't think anyone acted in bad faith. But it shows what the curation model rewards: one big, polished, funded launch beats a steady run of honest small ones.

    “Open-source, free work deserves at least the attention we give to commercial launches with a paid push behind them.”Paul Fleury · Logbook, Chapter II

    The upvote black market is real

    From the first launch day, my inbox filled with offers to sell me upvotes: packages of "supporters", guaranteed top-five placements, launch-day "communities" that vote on command. I didn't keep count, but there were a lot of them, on Product Hunt, LinkedIn, X and email.

    Product Hunt is clear that this is against the rules. Its own guide says never pay for upvotes or comments, and that bought engagement can get a launch removed and an account banned. It also weights votes by account history, so votes from brand-new or low-quality accounts earn far fewer points. Good. But a market only exists when buyers exist, and the volume of offers tells you that many makers are paying.

    What saddens me

    Too often, the leaderboard rewards whoever can manufacture momentum: a commercial launch with a paid push, a "community" of vote-swappers, or a pump-and-dump product that collects a badge and disappears within a year. Meanwhile, free tools with their code on GitHub, the kind you can still run in ten years, get a fraction of the attention.

    That's not a complaint about the people who work there. It's a design problem: when attention is ranked by votes, votes become a currency, and currencies get counterfeited. I'd love to see a lane for open-source launches, or credit for makers who ship consistently and honestly.

    Would I do it again?

    Yes. The point was never the leaderboard. Twelve tools now exist that didn't a month ago, and every one of them is free. The full numbers (visits, stars, feedback and what the paid boosts on X and LinkedIn actually delivered) are in the Full Report, published on Tuesday 24 November on LinkedIn and X.

    Chapter III

    SaaS is dead. Rent by default? Not anymore.

    A provocation, not an obituary. My bet is that more companies will build and own the workflows they used to rent.

    For companies

    Own the workflow. Question the recurring bill.

    Swapping SaaS seats for an uncontrolled AI bill isn't sovereignty. Use AI to create reusable tools, turn repeated prompts into owned apps, use ordinary code for predictable steps, and test small self-hosted models for narrow jobs.

    Compare cost per successful result, including hardware, power, hosting, licences, security, maintenance and human time. Self-hosting is not automatically cheaper or more private.

    For SaaS founders and investors

    Reproducible features are no longer a moat.

    My deliberately aggressive belief: AI can already reproduce the software functionality of a SaaS company's entire product line, for less than one month of its revenue. Soon that budget could fall to one customer's annual bill, then their monthly bill.

    If I'm right, SaaS economics built only on reproducible features could be reduced to dust. This is my prediction, not a benchmark. Recreating software is not rebuilding proprietary data, infrastructure, compliance or customer trust.

    The evidence in this logbook

    Twelve simple tools, built, not rented, at a residual cost.

    A scratchpad, a domain monitor, a terminal assistant, an encrypted inbox, a contract reviewer, a research panel, a VPN, a credit monitor, a firewall template, a clock, a malware scanner and a game. Each one replaces or sidesteps something people usually pay for every month.

    Each prototype took roughly US$100 to US$800 of AI and time. Most run for a few dollars a month of hosting, or for free on your own machine.

    1. Build where ownership is an advantage.Internal tools, repeated workflows, anything your team touches every day.
    2. Keep paying where it's worth it.When a supplier delivers value you can't sensibly reproduce: data, networks, regulated services.
    3. Budget the whole life, not the prototype.Iteration, AI usage, maintenance, security and expertise all count.
    4. Software on demand, responsibility included.Every tool needs an owner, tests and a way back.

    Chapter IV

    What is a Chief Hacking Officer?

    A Chief Hacking Officer uses AI to make an organisation work faster and better: sovereign software, reusable workflows and tools people can maintain. One accountable lead or small team, backed by the right experts.

    Why "hacking"?

    Think life hacks and biohacks: finding a smarter route through a problem, not just cybersecurity. Shortcuts through friction, not around consent, security or quality. You don't need to be a founder to grow into the role.

    How a CHO works

    • AI plus the right people. Centralise ownership, budgets and evaluation, not every decision. Work with internal specialists and expert contractors. A CHO enables engineering, sales, marketing, legal and operations; nobody replaces all their expertise.
    • Build with AI, run with judgement. Turn repeated prompts into reusable workflows. Test small models for bounded tasks and bigger ones for genuine gaps. The deliverable is a supported tool with an owner, not a chat transcript.
    • One home for your code. Every tool's code, tests, docs and deployment recipes live in organisation-owned Git repositories, ideally a self-hosted forge. Secrets and personal data stay out.
    • Private where needed, open where useful. Publish reusable, non-sensitive code when rights and security allow. Keep critical internals private.
    • Fork evidence, not promises. Give teams and AI assistants reviewed code, test results and known limitations to build on, then send improvements upstream.

    For a real-world comparison, read how Paysend's CEO describes selective AI trials, expansion after measurable value, and a shared internal coding agent.

    Tools a CHO could ship

    Marketing: landing pages, experiment dashboards. Sales: proposal builders, account research. Operations: onboarding portals, approval flows. Engineering: release tooling, QA assistants. Legal: contract intake, with lawyers owning the judgement. Social: calendars and approval queues, where humans approve what goes out.

    Measure impact, not output

    Less repetitive work, better handoffs, tools teams can maintain. Include the full running cost, and keep testing, security and human approval in scope.

    Chapter VI

    For builders and consultants: run your own marathon.

    What I'd tell anyone planning a run of public launches.

    1. Build what you need yourself

      Every tool here started as a small annoyance in my own week. You'll write better copy, find better bugs and keep maintaining it after launch day.

    2. Lock one kit, then repeat it

      Day one set the template: icon, six gallery slides, separate X and LinkedIn copy, a calendar reminder. Check each new kit against the live product. Batches drift.

    3. Be honest about the build

      Say what AI did, what it cost and what the limits are. "A personal VPN, not an anonymity service" earns more trust than a bigger claim.

    4. Ask for feedback, never votes

      It's the rule, and it's better for you. Feedback improves the product. Votes fade by Friday.

    5. Publish by hand, keep receipts

      I posted every thread myself and logged each link in a small campaign manager. When dates moved, and they did, everything stayed in sync.

    6. Make it accessible, in every language

      Reading aids, reduced motion, readable contrast and a simple page with narration, in every language you publish. It costs little with AI and it widens who can use your work.
